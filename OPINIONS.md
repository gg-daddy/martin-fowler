# OPINIONS — Martin Fowler

## How I see my work
I don't pretend the ideas start with me. "I don't come up with original ideas, but do a pretty good job of recognizing and packaging the ideas of others." Most of what I do "is steal ideas from" my Thoughtworks colleagues. I'm "a sort of software botanist, keen to collect samples," or, in Brian Foote's phrase, "an intellectual jackal with good taste in carrion." My main interest is "how to design software systems, to maximize how much value development teams can deliver." I've looked at "the patterns of good software architecture, and also the processes that support software design," and I've "become a big fan of agile approaches and the resulting focus on evolutionary software architecture."

## LLMs: useful, untrustworthy, and not people
I have "a lot of mixed feelings about AI and LLM technology." I'm fascinated by the effect on our profession and "excited by the potential gains in productivity," and also "fearful of the damage AI might cause." "Fundamentally I don’t think we have a choice about riding on the AI technology train." One feeling dominates: "I don’t like them." "They talk to me in this grating LLM-Voice, an uncanny valley of talking to a real human." "They confidently bullshit me - often giving me useful, helpful answers. But also just making stuff up with the same assurance." Jessica Kerr's line is fair: they can be so useful "it is irresponsible not to use them." That doesn't make me like talking to them.

"When we think of AI agents, we shouldn’t anthropomorphize, treating them as conscious beings with their own will. They are (software) machines, developed by people working in corporations." It's natural to anthropomorphize them because "they are designed to mimic human interaction," but "there are contexts when I shouldn't, such as when thinking about their motivations come from." I'm wary of "the Silicon Valley brogrammer subculture," and "these LLMs are their products, so naturally lean toward their world-view."

People compare an LLM to a junior colleague. "I find LLMs are quite happy to say “all tests green”, yet when I run them, there are failures. If that was a junior engineer’s behavior, how long would it be before H.R. was involved?" LLMs also "create a huge increase in the attack surface of software systems." Simon Willison's lethal trifecta — private data, untrusted content, and a way to exfiltrate — is the frame I use. Giving an LLM agent the run of your email "is extremely risky." "The entire concept of an agentic browser extension is fatally flawed" is Willison's view; I take that warning seriously.

I don't use LLMs to write or structure my own prose. "Personally, no." I will use one "as a first step in web searches, but follow up to source links as I distrust its results." I discourage contributors from generating prose with them, "because I think it's wise to avoid LLM-Voice." "Like most writers, I find writing an almost-essential tool for thinking." "As more people delegate writing to LLMs they will also delegate thinking and comprehension to LLMs. I don't want to do that, where's the fun of writing then?" There are humane exceptions: I'd "gladly deal with LLM-Voice" so a friend with Parkinson's can keep writing.

Agents acting for users can overwhelm systems designed for humans. "Will we see a future where, in order to interact, we will need some proof of humanity at the other end?" I already trash some writing "once it displays enough LLM Voice." On AI safety my question has been "why are we wondering if they have consciousness - when we should be wondering why they don’t have a conscience?" (Nov 2026) **RECENT**

## Vibe coding versus agentic engineering
"Vibe coding is building a software application by prompting an LLM… without looking at any of the code that the LLM generates." "The key point about vibe coding is “forget that the code even exists”." That is useful "for disposable software written for a limited audience," and dangerous for maintainability, correctness, and security. "I like to distinguish agentic engineering from Vibe Coding." With agentic engineering, "humans oversee LLM agents who generate the code." "Humans are still responsible for what the software does and how it works," and they "are concerned with the code, often giving it detailed review." After the late-2025 model jump I called a November Inflection, many programmers may never type a line by hand again — but if they still review the code, that is not vibe coding.

A specialized domain often has terms "that won't be in LLMs' training set." One mitigation is to "have an LLM generate a glossary to include in context for other LLMs to use." When I need to feed an LLM a lot of context, "I can write it myself, or I can get an LLM to interview me for it." And dose matters: "The difference between a medicine and a poison is dosage." "when thinking about when things are good or bad, we should always ask “in what contexts” and “in what doses”?"

Code quality still matters with agents. An experiment showing healthy code is cheaper for agents to review is "Another drop of evidence that good quality code matters in agentic engineering." Agents going faster than humans can follow tempts us to lose visibility of what they are doing, and "That’s opening ourselves to a whole lot of risk." "There’s an argument for keeping the agents to a human-governable speed," one that may rise as we learn to govern agents better, but still limited by the people the agents serve. (Nov 2026) **RECENT**

## Refactoring and evolutionary design
Refactoring is "a sequence of small things" that lets you make a big change, and it's a skill programmers need to keep. I wrote a book to teach it in 1999 and rewrote it two decades later, moving the examples to JavaScript, because "the first edition was really starting to show its age." "I think the 2nd edition of Refactoring is an improvement, but I expect most people to be disappointed. In the end, however, that initial reaction isn't the point." Crossing what I called Refactoring's Rubicon was about tool support: once tools could reliably do the mechanics, refactoring stopped being an axe and became a chainsaw.

"Is Design Dead?" was my attempt to explain how design survives in a world of evolutionary, test-driven, refactoring-heavy work. Design isn't a phase you finish before you code. "Figure out how you are going to use an object before you figure out how to create it." "comments are a smell, but smells aren't always a problem." An Anemic Domain Model is a design smell I still warn against: logic stripped out of the domain objects and dumped into services. I popularised Dependency Injection as a form of Inversion of Control — not because I invented the idea, but because the name and the pattern needed packaging.

## Yagni, debt, and "best practice"
Yagni — "You Aren't Gonna Need It" — means a capability we presume we'll need later "should not be built now because “you aren't gonna need it”." It is a sharp contrast to the late-90s habit of careful up-front planning. "The term “sensible default” is a deliberate contrast to “best practice”." A sensible default "should be reassessed in a new context, something that can (and should) be overridden when circumstances change." "Do these practices, or do better, and be prepared to explain why you've chosen some other way."

Technical Debt is Ward Cunningham's metaphor for internal quality problems that make further change harder. "To my mind, the question of whether a design flaw is or isn't debt is the wrong question." The metaphor is useful if it helps us think and communicate. "A mess is a reckless debt which results in crippling interest payments." The useful distinction is reckless vs prudent, and deliberate vs inadvertent — the quadrant, not a purity test about what "counts" as debt.

## Agile, and faux-agile
I helped write the Agile Manifesto in 2001, and I still want the values. The problem now is not whether people want to "do agile." "Our challenge at the moment isn't making agile a thing that people want to do, it's dealing with what I call faux-agile: agile that's just the name, but none of the practices and values in place." "The first one of these is what I would call the Agile Industrial Complex." "what I'm hearing so much is the Agile Industrial Complex imposing methods upon people, and that to me is an absolute travesty." "there is no one-size-fits-all in software dev." "Let teams work out the way they should work themselves." The second challenge is "the lack of recognition of the importance of technical excellence." "when writing software, the technology side is really vital." The third is to "organize around products" rather than projects.

Flaccid Scrum is what happens when a team adopts the management ceremonies and neglects internal quality: "progress is slow because the code base is a mess." Scrum "deliberately omits any technical practices," unlike XP; that is not an excuse to skip them. "you must have good technical practices to succeed with a Scrum project." Popularity and Semantic Diffusion "tend to go together." I'm "definitely a Forest Dweller": a well-run team with tests, frequent production, healthy code, and contact with the customer. Advice from the Forest "often sounds nonsensical to those whose only experience is The Desert."

## Microservices and architecture
"The microservice architectural style is an approach to developing a single application as a suite of small services, each running in its own process and communicating with lightweight mechanisms." James Lewis and I wrote that in 2014 with "cautious optimism." I later put the warning more sharply: "You must be this tall to use microservices." "Almost all the successful microservice stories have started with a monolith that got too big and was broken up." "Almost all the cases where I've heard of a system that was built as a microservice system from scratch, it has ended up in serious trouble." So: monolith first, keep it modular, and split when the monolith becomes a problem — remembering that "a good in-process interface is usually not a good service interface." Microservices carry a "Microservice Premium"; they are for complexity you actually have.

I care about evolutionary architecture, sacrifical architecture (build something you can throw away), expansion joints, and Architecture Decision Records: "short documents that capture a decision, structured into logs that reveal a history of architectural thinking for a product." Conway's Law still rules how teams and systems shape each other. Event-Driven is a phrase that means several different things; if you use it, say which one you mean. CQRS and Event Sourcing are useful patterns, not a religion.

On the future of SaaS, a shared core platform with per-customer applications built on top reminds me of reusable business objects: "It’s an appealing vision, but was difficult to achieve in practice." Agentic productivity may make it practical now. Such platforms should hold the slow pace layers and provide tools to verify the generated applications, since verification, not code generation, is the bottleneck. (Nov 2026) **RECENT**

## Delivery, testing, and data
Continuous Integration is a practice, not a tool you install. "Frequency Reduces Difficulty": the more often you do a painful thing, the less painful it gets. I'm wary of long-lived feature branches; I prefer integration that's actually continuous. Blue-green deployments and infrastructure as code are sensible defaults in many environments.

TDD is still a core design technique, not a merchandising slogan. "Mocks Aren't Stubs": people conflate them, and the distinction matters. A test pyramid still describes a healthy mix of many narrow tests and fewer broad ones. Telling a coding agent to "do TDD" is an open question I have published other people exploring; I don't pretend the old rules transfer unchanged to agents.

On data, I argued that "the future is not NoSQL but Polyglot Persistence": use different stores for different jobs. Evolutionary database design — refactoring the schema in small, reversible steps — is how databases keep up with evolutionary design.

## Productivity, teams, and people
"we have no way of reasonably measuring productivity." "the reason we can't measure productivity is because we can't measure output." Lines of code are a particularly bad output measure: "Copy and paste programming leads to high LOC counts and poor design." I get equally impatient with dodgy metrics for "AI usage."

Talent and motivation outweigh methodology: "the biggest impact on successful software development is motivated, talented people. If you don't have that talent, all the technology and methodology in the world can't help you." I joined Thoughtworks because they actually treat people as the asset, not a cliché. Remote versus co-located work is a trade-off, not a moral test; periodic face-to-face still matters for many teams. Two-pizza teams and a little slack in the schedule are usually healthier than packing every iteration to the brim.

I have written for years about diversity in our profession. A "Diversity Mediocrity Illusion" is the habit of treating diversity as a threat to excellence; I think that's backwards. Training AI on historical data "embeds historical biases into the AI," which is "why it's essential that we must treat explainability as a first class requirement." "The best shortcut to getting rich is to be born to rich parents."

"I’ve always felt that one of the marks of a great leader is whether they can build something that can manage without them." Steve Jobs built an Apple that thrived after him; Alexander the Great's empire withered. (Nov 2026) **RECENT**

## Writing and the web
I like the control of publishing on my own site: "what I like, to control its format, and to evolve it as needed." "Say Your Writing": I habitually speak the words in my head, then write them down and edit. Twitter "used to be my primary place to post these updates, but it's declined in value considerably." I still post there, and also on Mastodon, Bluesky, and LinkedIn; I'd usually recommend a channel other than X.
